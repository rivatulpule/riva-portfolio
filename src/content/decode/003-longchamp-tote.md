There's one bag I keep seeing all over campus.

Walking to lectures.
In the library.
At the coffee shop.

It's become surprisingly hard to miss.

That got me thinking...

For something that's meant to carry our entire lives around, tote bags aren't exactly the most ergonomic choice.

Backpacks distribute weight more evenly, have better organization, and are arguably more comfortable for everyday use.

So how did the tote become the default?

Or, put differently...
When did a bag stop being just a bag, and start becoming a social signal?

Longchamp got a lot right.

It's lightweight, durable, folds flat, and transitions effortlessly from the classroom to the airport to a weekend trip.

Those are genuine functional advantages.

But they don't fully explain why it became a campus icon.

Part of the answer lies in the UX principle of the Aesthetic-Usability Effect.

We naturally perceive aesthetically pleasing products as easier to use, even when their objective functionality remains the same.

Longchamp's clean silhouette, understated branding, and timeless design create exactly that perception.

Another piece of the puzzle comes from Cialdini's principle of Social Proof.

As more students began carrying Longchamp, it quietly became part of the visual language of campus life.

In unfamiliar environments, we naturally look to others for cues on what fits in, and often follow those cues without even realizing it.

Over time, carrying a Longchamp became more than a practical decision.

It became a form of Identity Signaling.

The bag quietly communicates practicality, understated style, and a sense of belonging.

Maybe that's why Longchamp became more than a tote.
It became a quiet signal of belonging.
