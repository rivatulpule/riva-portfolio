Most companies spend millions trying to make things easier for customers.
LEGO does something slightly different.

It hands you hundreds, sometimes thousands, of pieces and makes you do the work yourself.

And that effort might actually be part of what makes LEGO so valuable.

In behavioral science, there's a phenomenon called the IKEA Effect: we tend to place greater value on things we've helped create.

And here's where it gets interesting: LEGO was actually used in the original research behind the effect. Researchers found that participants who successfully assembled LEGO valued their creations more than those who hadn't built them.

But there was a catch.
When participants couldn't finish what they were building, that increase in value disappeared.

It wasn't just effort that mattered. It was effort + successful completion.

That makes LEGO particularly interesting from a business perspective. Most companies try to remove effort from the customer experience. LEGO has built an entire product experience around it. You sort the pieces. Follow the instructions. Build it step by step. And by the time the final piece clicks into place, you haven't just bought a product. You've invested your own time and effort into creating it.

This becomes even more interesting as LEGO expands into elaborate, display-worthy sets for adults, from flowers and architecture to cars and Star Wars.

The hours spent building aren't necessarily friction to eliminate. They're part of the value being created.
